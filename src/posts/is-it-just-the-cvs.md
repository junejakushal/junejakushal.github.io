In [The Letter to Cornell](/blog/the-letter-to-cornell), I wrote about why Feynman stayed at Caltech: a place where you could stumble into a biology student halfway through explaining an experiment, and leave with an idea you had not gone looking for. The ideas and the smart people found you, as long as you were somewhere they could.

Part of what kept those collisions coming was teaching. Teaching was not a cost he paid to do physics. It was part of what kept him able to do it.

---

The reasoning behind applying to CRISP was short: what the heck, let's apply.

Limited downside. Unknown upside. Take it.

Four months later, the question is a different one: how the heck did I get through this?

---

On paper, the job is simple. XLRI's [2019 convocation report](https://acad.xlri.ac.in/xluploads/convo/2019/files/basic-html/page90.html) describes it like this:

> CRISP is a committee at XLRI that undertakes the entire process of preparation of students for their placement processes, namely the summer internship process for first-year students and the lateral and final placement processes for second-year students.

Every B-school in India has some version of it, a sister committee to the placement committee. At IIM Lucknow it is called Disha.

The first two activities on its list are about CVs: mapping each candidate to a senior "with similar profiles so as to increase inter-batch interaction," and "ensuring the authenticity of information provided on the CVs through a meticulous data verification process."

So on paper, you only have to make sure the CVs get made, the prep gets done, and last year's interview experiences get passed down. That is the whole description.

What the description leaves out is the committee doing it. Nineteen people. Two campuses, Jamshedpur and Delhi. Two streams of students, BM and HR. At any given moment, Delhi might be handling Jamshedpur's work, Jamshedpur might be handling Delhi's, or both might be untangling something together. CRISP is the only committee that has to work across campuses just to keep its everyday work running.

And nobody joins it for the CV. There is no fund to manage, no event with tens of thousands of footfall to put in a highlight line. Just nineteen very different people who volunteered, setting their egos and their sleep aside so that the placement machinery of a B-school keeps chugging along.

---

When I arrived at XL, my mentor and my verifier were the only seniors I knew.

They were the bridge. First for summer internship prep: which companies, how the process works, what the training is for. Then for academics. Later, for everything else.

There are 600 people in a batch. In the one year our batches overlapped, I knew only two of them. The ones that CRISP mapped to me.

This year I got to stand where my CRISP mentor stood. I was assigned thirty juniors, from every part of India and a few from outside it. For many of them, like me a year ago, I might be the only senior they actually talk to.

To some, I am someone who helps resolve blockers. To some, a bridge to a wider network. To some, someone to tolerate through a couple of mandatory meetings until the summer process is over.

---

When your mentees are away on UNURUM.

They are on the village immersion, or on Leadership Immersion, somewhere with no electricity, where a storm or a flood has brought the network poles down. Their CV points still need to be approved. You are on campus, and there is no way to reach them.

The deadline is in an hour.

So you walk the second-year dorms and wake the verifiers, your own batchmates, out of their sleep or whatever else they are in the middle of, and sit down with them to triage the points and the proofs before the clock runs out.

Is it just the CVs?

---

When mom sends homemade sweets.

It is the middle of placement season. Your mentees are chasing summer offers, solving cases, sitting interviews, keeping up with academics, attending company presentations, networking, building a personal brand. Almost everything in their week is something to perform.

So you keep a regular catch-up with them, and one evening you bring a cake, some KitKats, and the sweets mom sent from home.

The sweets are the simplest thing on the table. They are the first to finish.

It is not the room that changes. It is the people in it. Their whole attitude shifts, suddenly, and even now you cannot quite describe how.

Is it just the CVs?

---

When juniors travel from Delhi to attend a presentation.

It starts with convincing them to come to Jamshedpur at all, just to present their idea. Then you stay on call with them while they sort it out with their dean, their professors and the academic office, so that missing classes does not cost them a grade.

Then they travel. Three hours from college to the airport. A delay. A three-hour flight. Another delay. Three more hours by road to the Jamshedpur campus, through the elephant corridor of Jharkhand.

Your job is to make sure they are in the presentation room at 9 AM. How they get there from another city, how they handle the logistics, none of that is your business. It is not your JTBD, your job to be done.

Yet you help them settle in. You join them for dinner. Maybe you sit through their presentation as a listener and play devil's advocate.

Is it just the CVs?

---

When a junior gets hurt in their dorm.

Their batchmates have classes, placement activities, a dozen reasons to be somewhere else. None of them help.

Surely it is just the CVs.

Then why are their mentor and co-mentor the ones sitting with them in the hospital?

---

When the team prints more than 6,000 CVs.

Every one of them gets stamped. Every one goes into a folder. Then the folders go out, one to every junior, by hand, with the same three words each time: you've got this.

Is it just the CVs?

---

Then D-Day comes, and there is nothing left to do.

You get them mints before the personal interview. After it, sour candy, a Chupa Chups, anything to bring the nerves down.

It is their interview. Their group discussion. You cannot walk into the room with them, and nothing you say in the corridor changes what happens inside it.

But you still have to be there.

So you are. When the process starts at 3 AM, and when it ends at 3 AM the next day. In person. Online.

---

So, is it just the CVs?

Maybe that is what CRISP is, at the end of the day: a student-first body. Probably the only thing in a B-school that puts the student's interests first, and does not treat them as a statistic: a source of fees, a placement number, a line in next year's report.

It is not just the CVs. Any agent can do that for you.

---

Feynman needed the teaching to keep doing the physics. I suspect the thirty did the same for me.

In the Cornell cafeteria, Feynman was not the one who threw the plate. Someone else did. All he did was watch it closely enough to notice the wobble.

This year there were thirty plates in the air, and none of them were mine to throw. The job was to notice which ones were wobbling, and to know when to steady them and when to let them spin.
